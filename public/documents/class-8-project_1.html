The Class 8 Project

The first great independent work of a Waldorf education — a year-long study of the student's own choosing that closes the eight-year journey with the class teacher

I.What the Class 8 Project Is

In most Waldorf schools across the world, the eighth grade year — the final year of the lower school, the year before high school — is crowned by a substantial year-long independent project. Each student chooses a topic of their own. They research it. They learn a new skill connected to it. They make something tangible. They write a paper about it. And then they stand before their school community and present what they have done.

This is the Class 8 Project — sometimes called the 8th Grade Project, the Eighth Grade Project, or simply "the Project." Like the Class 12 Senior Project that some students will encounter four years later, the Class 8 Project is one of the distinctive rites of passage of a Waldorf education. It is the first substantial piece of independent academic and practical work a Waldorf student undertakes — the formal moment they are asked to leave behind the receiving consciousness of childhood and take up the active consciousness of someone who can choose, sustain, and complete a major undertaking of their own.

For many Waldorf students, this is the project they remember for the rest of their school years. It is often the first time they have been asked to be in charge of something significant — not in collaboration with classmates, not under the close direction of a teacher, but as the central author of their own substantial work.

A note on names Different schools call it different things: the Class 8 Project, the 8th Grade Project, the Eighth Grade Project, sometimes "the project," sometimes the Class 8 Special Project. The form is consistent across schools even when the name varies.

II.Why Class 8 — The Right Age for This Work

The placement of this project at age thirteen or fourteen is not accidental. It meets the eighth-grader at a particular moment in their development — a moment when they are newly capable of substantial independent work and newly hungry to prove they can do it.

The fourteen-year-old's question

The Class 8 student is, in many ways, no longer a child. They are crossing the threshold of adolescence; their bodies and minds are reshaping; they are beginning to feel themselves as separate individuals with interests, passions, and questions that belong to them. Steiner and the broader Waldorf tradition describe this as the moment the child begins to differentiate strongly from the surrounding adults — to test the world, to push against received ideas, to look for who they themselves are becoming.

The Class 8 Project meets this developmental moment directly. It says to the student: you have interests of your own; we recognise them; show us what you can do with them. The project does not impose a topic; it asks the student to bring one. This recognition — that the student has become someone with genuine interests worth respecting — is itself part of the project's gift.

The end of the eight-year journey

The Class 8 Project also marks something else: the end of the eight-year journey with the class teacher. In the traditional Waldorf model, the same teacher accompanies the class from Class 1 through Class 8 — eight years with one teacher who has known the student deeply, taught them across all subjects, watched them grow from a six-year-old into a young teenager. The Class 8 Project is the student's last substantial work under this teacher's guidance. It is, in a sense, their thank-you and their goodbye.

This is part of what makes the project feel weighty. The eighth grader is not just completing an assignment; they are showing their class teacher — and themselves, and their family — what eight years of Waldorf education has built in them. The project is the visible answer to the unspoken question: what has this education made me capable of?

What the project quietly builds

  • Independence. For the first time in their school life, the student is in charge of a substantial piece of work over a long time. They cannot wait for the teacher to tell them what to do next; they have to figure out the next step themselves
  • Stamina. A year is a long time to live with a single topic. The project asks the student to sustain interest, push through difficulty, return to the work week after week
  • Public speaking. The presentation at the end is often the first time the student speaks formally before a community audience. Many graduates name this as the experience that gave them confidence in public speaking for life
  • Adult relationships. Through the mentor, the student has their first sustained working relationship with an adult professional outside the family and school
  • The bringing of inner interest into outer form. Something the student has loved internally — an instrument, a sport, an animal, a craft — becomes a real piece of work in the world. This is itself a profound experience for a fourteen-year-old

III.The Three Pillars — Knowledge, Skill, Tangible Product

Many Waldorf schools structure the Class 8 Project around three explicit pillars — three components that together make the project complete. Whether or not a school names them this way, these three elements are usually present.

Pillar 1 Gaining Knowledge Substantial research into the topic — reading, interviews, study. The student becomes genuinely knowledgeable in their area.
Pillar 2 Learning a New Skill A new capacity acquired in the doing — a craft, a technique, a practice. The student becomes able to do something they could not do before.
Pillar 3 Creating a Tangible Product Something concrete that comes out of the work — an object made, a performance given, a body of work produced. The project leaves a visible trace.

The three pillars together ensure the project is not merely an academic exercise (Pillar 1 alone), not merely a craft project (Pillar 2 alone), and not merely a product display (Pillar 3 alone). Instead, the student must integrate thinking, doing, and making — the same head-heart-hand integration that runs through the entire Waldorf curriculum, now brought together in a single substantial work.

What this means in practice

A student researching traditional Japanese sword-fighting would (Pillar 1) read about the history of bushido, the development of the katana, the philosophy of the samurai; (Pillar 2) learn actual sword forms from a martial-arts instructor mentor; and (Pillar 3) demonstrate one or two forms in the final presentation, perhaps alongside displaying a wooden practice sword they have learned to handle.

A student researching beekeeping would (Pillar 1) study the biology of bees, the history of beekeeping, the structure of a hive; (Pillar 2) learn to handle a hive with a beekeeper mentor through a full season; and (Pillar 3) bring honey from their own work, photographs of the hive, perhaps a frame of comb, to the final presentation.

A student writing a children's book would (Pillar 1) study children's literature, the structure of picture books, the craft of illustration; (Pillar 2) develop their own writing and illustration skills with a writer or artist mentor; and (Pillar 3) produce a complete, illustrated, bound children's book.

IV.The Shape of the Year

Different schools structure the Class 8 Project differently — some begin at the end of Class 7, some at the start of Class 8; some spread the project across the full school year, others concentrate it in two terms. The following is a typical pattern.

The project year at a glance

  • End of Class 7 / start of Class 8: students choose their topic; class teacher approves
  • Early autumn: mentor secured; initial research begins
  • Autumn through mid-winter: deep research; regular mentor meetings; first written drafts; skill-learning underway
  • Mid-winter through spring: tangible product takes shape; paper drafted and revised; classroom check-ins continue
  • Late spring: rehearsal of presentations; final paper due; product completed
  • Late spring / early summer: public presentations, usually over one or two evenings
  • Soon after: Class 8 graduation, where the project is often referenced

Stage by stage

Stage 1 — Choosing the topic

The first hard question

The single most important step — and often the most difficult. The class teacher usually opens this conversation near the end of Class 7 or in the first weeks of Class 8. What do you want to learn about? What have you always been curious about? What would you regret never learning?

Some students arrive immediately with a clear answer. Others spend weeks circling possibilities. Both responses are normal. The teacher helps the student find the right scope — not so narrow the topic runs out of material, not so broad the project cannot be finished, not so easy the student is not stretched, not so difficult the student is overwhelmed.

A short written proposal usually follows — sometimes a single page, sometimes more. The proposal names the topic, the planned skill, the intended tangible product, the proposed mentor (if known), and an initial reading list. The class teacher approves the proposal before serious work begins.

Stage 2 — Finding the mentor

Connecting with the outside world

The mentor is a person outside the school faculty who is an expert in the student's chosen field. This is the project's first real connection to the wider adult world — and for many students, the first time they have approached a working adult on their own to ask for serious help.

Mentors come from everywhere: family friends, neighbours, local craftspeople, professionals contacted through a parent's network, members of the school community with relevant expertise. The class teacher often helps connect students to mentors, but the student is usually expected to make initial contact themselves — write the introductory email, make the phone call, ask the question.

This is genuinely formative. Many Class 8 students later describe the moment they sent the first email to their mentor as their first experience of being treated as someone with serious work to do.

Stage 3 — The long middle

Research, skill-learning, making

The bulk of the year. The student reads, takes notes, develops their argument. They meet their mentor regularly — monthly is typical, sometimes more often in intensive periods. They begin learning the new skill. They begin making the tangible product. They take photographs of their progress, keep a journal of the work, write up findings.

This is the part of the project where most students hit a wall — usually around mid-winter, when the initial enthusiasm has worn off and the finish is still months away. The class teacher's role here is crucial: keeping the student moving, asking the right questions, helping them through the periods of doubt and difficulty.

Stage 4 — The paper

The written record

The Class 8 research paper is typically 5 to 15 pages, depending on the school's expectations and the student's project. Some schools require a longer paper for stronger writers and a shorter one for those still developing their writing. The paper is not necessarily expected to meet adult academic standards — it is a substantial piece of writing by a fourteen-year-old, and it is judged as such.

What is expected: a clear introduction, structured body chapters or sections, a conclusion that reflects on what was learned, proper citations or footnotes (often the student's first encounter with academic citation), and a bibliography. Many schools also expect a personal reflection section — what surprised the student, what they found difficult, what they came to understand differently.

Stage 5 — The tangible product

The thing made or done

Running parallel to the writing is the making. The student finishes their bookshelf, completes their illustrated book, refines their dance, finalises their hand-built kayak, paints the last canvas. The tangible product is usually displayed alongside the presentation; sometimes the presentation itself is a demonstration.

Real Class 8 tangible products from recent presentations include: a renovated motorbike, a self-illustrated children's book, homemade catapults, a hand-crafted hammock, a built-from-scratch computer, a complete reimagined skincare brand with packaging, a stop-motion Lego film, a wood-strip canoe, a knife forged from raw steel, a fully sewn formal dress.

Stage 6 — The presentation

Standing before the community

The presentation is the culminating event — usually a 15 to 25 minute formal lecture given to an audience of family, classmates, teachers, mentors, and community members. Many schools hold these over one, two, or three evenings, with three to seven students presenting per night. The student speaks about their topic, demonstrates their skill where possible, shows their tangible product, and takes questions from the audience.

For many fourteen-year-olds, this is the most exposed they have ever been in public. It is also, for many, the moment they realise they can do something they did not know they could do.

V.The Mentor Relationship

The mentor is one of the gifts of the Class 8 Project — a working adult outside the school who agrees to guide the student through their year-long work. The mentor is not the student's teacher; they are something closer to a small apprentice's master, or an experienced colleague generously giving time.

What a mentor does

  • Meets with the student regularly — typically once a month, sometimes more often in intensive periods of skill-learning or making
  • Teaches the new skill — directly or by guiding the student to practice
  • Shares their professional knowledge — opens up the texture of a working life in the field
  • Answers questions — both practical (how to handle a hive, how to lay out a comic book panel) and philosophical (what got you into this work, what does mastery feel like)
  • Reviews drafts and the tangible product — provides feedback at the level of someone who actually knows what they're doing
  • Attends the final presentation — and often speaks publicly about the experience of mentoring the student

Where mentors come from

  • School faculty members with relevant expertise (less common — mentors are usually outside the school)
  • Parents of other students in the school with relevant professional skills
  • Family friends, neighbours, members of the local community
  • Professionals contacted directly by the student — local artists, craftspeople, business owners, scientists, scholars
  • Sometimes mentors found through a parent's professional network
  • Occasionally remote mentors via correspondence or video calls — when a particular expertise cannot be found locally
What the mentor relationship offers Beyond the practical help with the project, the mentor offers something rarer: the experience of being treated by a working adult as someone with serious work to do. For a fourteen-year-old, this is genuinely formative. Many Class 8 students later describe their mentor relationship as one of the most important adult relationships of their school years — sometimes lasting well into the student's adult life, occasionally shaping the student's eventual career.

VI.What Students Actually Choose — Real Topic Lists

One of the most striking things about Class 8 Project lists from real Waldorf schools is the genuine range of what fourteen-year-olds choose when given the freedom. The following topics are drawn from published project lists at Waldorf schools in North America and beyond — real projects undertaken by real students in recent years.

Practical making & building

  • How to make a go-kart
  • Building a bookshelf
  • Renovating a vintage motorbike
  • Building a wood-strip canoe or kayak
  • Making a sail
  • Making a knife
  • Building a computer from parts
  • Building a hand-crafted hammock
  • Designing and building homemade catapults
  • Making an African djembe drum

Sports, outdoor activities & physical skill

  • Becoming a scuba diver
  • The art and practice of karate
  • Japanese sword-fighting (kendo)
  • The history and practice of rock climbing
  • Fly fishing (with a local fly-shop owner as mentor)
  • Designing and building a terrain park feature (skiing)
  • Gymnastics and the relationship to autism
  • Horsemanship — learning to handle and train a horse

Arts, writing & creative work

  • Writing and illustrating a children's book
  • Writing a comic book
  • Authentic prop-making (for theatre or film)
  • Fashion design — researching and sewing original pieces
  • The art of photography
  • Outdoor adventure filmmaking
  • Stop-motion animation with Legos
  • Writing fiction — building a fantasy world from scratch
  • Political cartooning and editorial illustration

Food & the working of materials

  • The art and history of sushi (and learning to make it)
  • The art of making ice cream
  • Sourdough bread across the seasons
  • Honey and beekeeping
  • Cheesemaking
  • Chocolate from bean to bar

Social issues & the wider world

  • The Holocaust — interviewing survivors and sharing their stories
  • Girls' education in developing countries
  • Climate change — research, action, and advocacy
  • Homelessness in the student's home city
  • Plastic recycling — research and a community project
  • Earthquake preparedness
  • Overcoming adversity — interviews with people who have faced significant challenges

Science, biology, mind

  • A study of colorblindness
  • Training an autism service dog
  • Astronomy — the planets, the stars, what we can see from earth
  • The physiology of a chosen sport
  • An ecology project — documenting a single small habitat across a year
  • The science of fermentation
  • Marine biology — a single tide pool studied through the seasons

Reimagining a small business or product

  • Designing a complete skincare brand — formulation, packaging, marketing
  • Starting a small soap-making business
  • Designing and printing a small book of poetry
  • Creating a complete photography portfolio with marketing

What unites them

Looking across this range, there is no single subject area that defines a Class 8 Project. What unites the topics is something deeper: each is something the student cares about enough to live with for a year. The very breadth of the topics is itself a sign of what the project does — it draws out what is uniquely the student's, not what the school has prescribed. Two students in the same Class 8 may produce wildly different projects, and both are exactly right.

A note on topic selection The best Class 8 Project topics are usually neither the most ambitious nor the most safe. A student who chooses something far beyond their capacity may finish disappointed; a student who chooses something they already know well misses the project's point. The right topic stretches the student — into territory they don't yet know but feel they could enter, with help. Trust your child's instincts here, but help them aim for the territory that feels both interesting and slightly daunting.

VII.The Research Paper

Every Class 8 Project includes a substantial written paper — typically the student's most ambitious piece of writing to date.

What the paper usually contains

  • An introduction — naming the topic, explaining what drew the student to it, posing the question they have explored
  • Body chapters or sections — usually 3 to 5, each addressing one aspect of the topic; for a sports project, this might be history, technique, the practitioners, the inner experience of the sport
  • A description of the practical work undertaken — the skill learned, the tangible product made, the process of learning, the difficulties met
  • Personal reflection — what surprised the student, what they found difficult, what they came to understand differently. This is usually the most genuinely interesting part of a Class 8 paper
  • A conclusion — reflecting on the journey as a whole, naming what comes next
  • A bibliography — usually 5 to 15 sources for a Class 8 project, properly cited
  • Photographs and illustrations — most schools encourage substantial visual content, particularly for practical projects

Expected length and standards

Class 8 papers are typically 5 to 15 pages of typed text, sometimes longer for students whose project has produced extensive material. Schools vary in their standards — some require strict academic formatting (proper footnotes, MLA or APA style), others accept more flexible structures. The class teacher sets the standard in keeping with what the class has been prepared for in their writing work across the year.

The paper is not graded the way a high-school paper might be. It is read by the class teacher, sometimes by an additional faculty reader, with feedback returned to the student in time for revision. The standard is "the best work this particular student can produce" rather than a universal academic benchmark.

VIII.The Public Presentation

The presentation is the public-facing culmination of the project — the moment the student stands before their community and shows what they have done.

The form

A typical Class 8 Project presentation runs 15 to 25 minutes, sometimes longer for projects with substantial demonstrations or performances. It is given before an audience that includes:

  • The student's immediate family — usually including grandparents and extended relatives where possible
  • The class teacher and other faculty members
  • The student's classmates (who typically attend each other's presentations)
  • The student's mentor — often invited to say a few words after the presentation
  • Members of the wider school community — parents, alumni, friends of the school

The presentation usually includes:

  • An opening that introduces the student, the topic, and why they chose it
  • The main lecture portion — what the student has learned, organised clearly
  • Visual aids — slides, photographs, examples, the tangible product on display
  • A demonstration where appropriate — playing a piece of music, performing a kata, showing the working catapult, slicing the sushi
  • A question-and-answer period — typically 5 to 10 minutes, with questions from the audience
  • Sometimes a closing remark from the class teacher or the mentor

The format of the evening

Most Waldorf schools hold Class 8 Project presentations over one to three evenings, with three to seven students presenting per evening. The evenings are typically substantial events — opened by the class teacher, attended by the full school community where possible, often followed by a reception. Families bring food. Younger siblings sit in the audience and watch their older siblings shine. The atmosphere is celebratory.

What the presentation gives the student

  • The experience of speaking formally before a real audience — often for the first time
  • The experience of being publicly recognised — by name, for substantial work, in front of family and community
  • The experience of fielding unscripted questions — a different and harder skill than the prepared lecture
  • The experience of being witnessed — by their family, teachers, classmates, mentor, school community, all gathered for this purpose
What was inner becomes outer. What was solitary becomes public. What the student has quietly become is, finally, named and seen by the community that has watched them grow up.

IX.The Class 8 Graduation Context

The Class 8 Project does not stand alone. It is part of a constellation of culminating events that together form the close of the lower-school journey.

What else happens at the end of Class 8

In a typical Waldorf school, the final weeks of Class 8 include several substantial events, often clustered closely together:

  • The Class 8 Project presentations — usually held in the spring (or late spring of the southern academic year)
  • The Class 8 Play — frequently a Shakespeare production (often A Midsummer Night's Dream, The Tempest, or Twelfth Night), or a major musical (Fiddler on the Roof, The Sound of Music). See our Class Plays by Grade guide for the full list
  • The Class 8 Trip — many schools take their eighth grade on a multi-day or week-long trip, often involving outdoor adventure, service work, or a journey to a meaningful destination
  • The Class 8 Graduation Ceremony — a formal ceremony in which the class teacher gives a substantial address to each student individually, often referencing the project, and presents their diploma
  • The class teacher's farewell — for those students whose class teacher has been with them since Class 1, this is the closing of an eight-year relationship

Why these things are bunched together

The clustering is deliberate. The end of Class 8 is a true threshold — the close of the lower school, the close of the class teacher era, the close of childhood proper. The Waldorf tradition honours this transition with substantial form: a project, a play, a trip, a graduation, a farewell. Together they mark the threshold in a way that few modern educational moments do.

The Class 8 Project sits at the heart of this constellation. It is the most personal of these culminating acts — the one that the student alone has produced — and so it carries particular weight as the formal expression of who they have become.

X.For Parents — Supporting the Year

The parent of a Class 8 student often wonders how best to support their child through this substantial year. Here is practical guidance from families who have been through it.

Help with logistics, not content

The temptation to help with the substance of the project is real — your child is struggling, the work is significant, you have skills they don't. Resist. The struggle is the project's gift. What you can genuinely help with is logistics: scheduling mentor meetings, driving to libraries or workshops, helping order materials for the tangible product, providing the steady evenings of work-time and dinner. These are real and important gifts.

Talk about it without taking it over

Your child needs to talk through their thinking — sometimes at length. Ask open questions. Listen patiently. Resist the urge to give answers. Sometimes the best parental response is "what do you think about that?" rather than offering a solution.

Be present, but not central

Show up to the presentation. Bring extended family. Take photographs of the work. Treat the project with the same seriousness with which the school is treating it — your child is learning what it feels like for adults to care about their work, and your stance shapes the whole experience.

Acknowledge what's hard

Many students hit a wall in mid-winter. Acknowledge it openly. This is hard. You're doing the difficult middle of something big. Most things worth doing feel like this in the middle. The reassurance that the wall is normal — not a sign of failure — often helps a fourteen-year-old keep going.

Don't compare

Different Class 8 students produce wildly different projects. The student who builds a wooden boat is not doing more or better than the student who writes a children's book. Resist any temptation to compare your child's project to a classmate's — out loud or even silently. The project that fits your child is the right project for them.

The role you actually play You are not the project's teacher; you are not its mentor; you are not its judge. You are the safe and steady presence that makes the project possible — the parent who drives to the workshop, makes the evening meal, listens to the doubts at bedtime, and is there in the audience the night of the presentation. This role is small in the project's structure and enormous in your child's experience of it. Don't underestimate what just showing up — quietly, steadily, lovingly — gives them.

XI.For Homeschool Families

Waldorf homeschool families can absolutely adapt the Class 8 Project tradition — and it is genuinely worth doing, even without a school community around it.

The basic adaptation

The structure of a homeschool Class 8 Project can mirror the school version almost exactly:

  • Student chooses a topic — with the parent helping to refine the scope
  • Parent helps the student find a mentor — almost always from outside the family. The act of writing to an unknown adult and asking for their time is a real part of the project's purpose
  • Three pillars apply — knowledge through research, a new skill learned, a tangible product made
  • A research paper — 5 to 15 pages depending on the student's writing capacity
  • A public presentation — to whatever audience can be gathered

The presentation audience for homeschoolers

The public presentation is the part that requires the most adaptation. Options:

  • An extended family gathering — formal invitations, grandparents and aunts and uncles in the audience, the project displayed with care
  • A homeschool co-op — if you are part of a homeschool community, gather everyone for a presentation evening; consider asking another Class 8 homeschool student to present alongside yours
  • The local Waldorf school — some schools welcome local homeschool Class 8 students to present at their school evenings, particularly where the family has been part of the wider Waldorf community
  • The local library, church hall, or community space — a formal venue, even a borrowed one, lends the event the weight it deserves
  • An online presentation with recording — a live audience of family and friends via video call, with the recording shared more widely afterwards

Why it matters even outside a school

The Class 8 Project — adapted for a homeschool family — gives the young teenager their first real experience of substantial independent work, of public presentation, of being witnessed by their community for a serious effort. These are formative experiences that the rest of life will ask of them, and the project offers them at exactly the right developmental moment.

Resist the temptation to make the project smaller or easier because your child is at home. The same standards that apply at a Waldorf school can apply at home — the mentor, the paper, the product, the presentation. Your child will rise to the bar you set.

XII.For Teachers — Guiding the Project

For class teachers preparing to guide their Class 8 students through the project, some practical guidance from experienced colleagues.

Begin early

Even if formal work begins in Class 8, raise the project as a possibility in Class 7. Let the students live with the idea for months before they have to commit to a topic. The best topic choices often come from interests the student has been carrying for years.

Help students find the right scope

The two most common failure modes are too big and too small. A topic too big leaves the student exhausted and unable to do justice to any part of it; a topic too small runs out of material by mid-winter. Help each student find the scope that matches their capacity — and accept that this will be different for every student in the class.

Lean on the mentor system

The mentor is the project's secret weapon. The student receives knowledge and guidance you cannot personally provide — they get an actual professional in the field. But this only works if the mentor relationship is real. Help students choose mentors carefully, draft their initial outreach emails together if needed, and check in throughout the year that mentor meetings are actually happening.

Build in early deadlines

Most students will procrastinate without scaffolding. Set a deadline for the proposal (month one), the mentor confirmation (month two), the first written section (month four), the first draft of the paper (month seven), and so on. The deadlines are gentle but real; they keep the project moving when motivation flags.

Plan the presentations carefully

The presentation evenings are major events. Schedule them well in advance. Print programmes. Set up the room formally. Invite the broader school community. Treat the evening with the dignity the work deserves — the students will rise to meet whatever atmosphere the school sets.

What you are teaching Through the Class 8 Project, you are teaching something that will last well beyond the school year. You are teaching your students that they can choose a substantial undertaking, sustain it across time, push through difficulty, and stand publicly behind what they have done. This is one of the most important things any human being learns. The topic of the project matters far less than the fact that the student has had this experience. Trust the form; the form will do its work.
