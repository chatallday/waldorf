I.The grain of the day

Each day of the week has its own grain, tied to a planet. This one is Waldorf tradition.

DayGrainWhat it's like
SundaySunWheatWarm and golden. Bread, mostly.
MondayMoonRiceWhite, soft and calming.
TuesdayMarsBarleyOld and sturdy. Good in soups and stews.
WednesdayMercuryMilletLight, quick to cook, bright yellow.
ThursdayJupiterRyeDark and hardy. The bread grain of northern Europe.
FridayVenusOatsSoothing. Porridge.
SaturdaySaturnCornHeavy and earthy. Polenta or cornbread.

Nobody follows this to the letter. Even doing it a few days a week gives the week a shape, and kids pick up on that.

II.Root, leaf, fruit

This one is Steiner's own. In two 1924 talks to the workmen, he linked root to the brain and nerves, leaf to the chest and breathing, and blossom and fruit to the digestion. Try to get all three each day.

Head · Thinking

Roots

Carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips, celeriac. Steiner said roots get worked on in the head, and that a tea made from a root acts on head complaints. Carrots are the Waldorf favorite.

Heart · Feeling

Leaves and stems

Spinach, kale, chard, lettuce, cabbage, herbs. The middle of the plant, linked to breathing and the chest.

Hands · Will

Flowers, fruits and seeds

Apples, berries, tomatoes, squash, nuts, and the grains and beans too. He said these get worked on in the gut, so the head is left free.

III.Bringing it home

The habits that come up most.

Daily

Whole grains

Porridge for breakfast, whole grain bread, grain soups. Cooked whole grains are the staple carbohydrate.

Where you can

Organic and biodynamic

Steiner started biodynamic farming in 1924. Buy organic or Demeter when it's within reach, and eat what's local and in season.

Plant-forward

Mostly vegetarian

Many Waldorf schools serve vegetarian meals. Steiner himself didn't hand out one diet for everyone, so meat, if you eat it, is a personal call. Keep it good quality and occasional.

Steiner

Go easy on potatoes

In 1923 he told the workmen that a diet leaning on potatoes was bad for thinking. His reasoning: the tuber isn't processed in the gut, so the head ends up doing the digesting. He preferred rye and wheat, and lentils, beans and peas. Not a ban, just don't live on them.

Steiner

Salt

He said salt isn't only for taste. It matters for thinking. Spices work in a similar way, so a little goes a long way.

Steiner

Four nutrients

He named four: protein, fats, carbohydrates and salts. He said you need all four, and that percentages on a chart don't tell you what a food does for a person.

Steiner

Honey and milk

In 1923 he talked about a children's home using honey stirred into warm milk for undernourished kids. He also said milk can't be rebuilt from its parts: mice fed casein, fat, sugar and salts did worse than mice fed milk.

Waldorf practice

Sweeten simply

Honey, fruit, maple syrup or rapadura in place of refined sugar. This is common practice in Waldorf kitchens. I didn't find a Steiner source for it.

Every meal

Make it a ritual

A blessing or verse before eating, a candle, a set table, no screens, no rushing. How you eat counts as much as what.

IV.Food at school

What it looks like in a Waldorf classroom.

Kindergarten

Snack

Children help make it: kneading dough, cutting fruit, washing vegetables. A verse comes first. Typical snacks are whole grain bread with butter and honey, seasonal fruit, soup, rice porridge or millet.

Grade 3 on

Garden

Most schools have one. From Grade 3 the children grow, harvest and cook their own food, and sometimes grind the grain.

Any grade

Bread baking

Grind, mix, knead, wait, bake, eat together. It comes up naturally in Grade 3 with farming and crafts, but works at any age.

V.Books

Start with the first two.

Wendy Cook

Foodwise

The standard anthroposophical nutrition book. Covers grains, the threefold plant, sweeteners, bread, dairy and more, with tips on menus and children's food.

Wendy Cook

The Biodynamic Food & Cookbook

Over 150 seasonal recipes, plus the thinking behind them.

Rudolf Steiner

Nutrition: Food, Health and Spiritual Development

Steiner's own lectures on food, gathered into one volume. Heavy going, but it's the source.

Practical

Simply in Season

Not Waldorf, but organized by season and built on local whole foods. Many Waldorf families use it.

Practical

Moosewood Cookbook

Whole food vegetarian cooking. A longtime favorite in Waldorf kitchens, though not anthroposophical.

Practical

Nourishing Traditions

Fermenting, sourdough, broths and traditional grain prep. Used in Waldorf circles, though it isn't Steiner-based.
