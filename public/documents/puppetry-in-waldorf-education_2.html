I.Why Puppetry Matters

A puppet show, in the Waldorf view, is a told story made visible — but only just. The figures move in soft, simple gestures, the scenery is a folded silk, and the rest is left for the child to imagine.

Puppetry has lived in Waldorf education since its very beginning. In 1917, two years before the first Waldorf school opened, Rudolf Steiner was living above a puppet theatre in Berlin, and the puppeteers there came to him for guidance on their craft. From that early advice grew a quiet but central tradition: the small, simple puppet plays that still open the day in Waldorf kindergartens, and the more ambitious puppetry that returns again and again through the grades. Steiner himself spoke of puppetry as something that could be "healing for the ravages of civilization."

Story softened, story made gentle

A puppet show shows just enough and no more. The figures glide rather than walk, faces are simple and barely changing, voices are quiet. This restraint, far from being a limitation, is exactly the gift: where a film or even a vivid picture book hands the child a finished image they must accept, a puppet show offers an outline that the child completes inwardly. The imagination is invited in, not displaced.

The image enters slowly

Stories told through puppets reach the young child without overwhelming them. The early-childhood educator Rahima Baldwin Dancy has noted that when fairy tales are translated into cartoons and films they lose their evocative quality and become too powerful for young children, while the same stories acted out with stand-up puppets or marionettes have a calming, healing effect. The pace of a puppet play is the pace at which a small child can take a picture in.

Reverence — for what is small and slow

A puppet show in a Waldorf kindergarten is set up with care: a small table, a draped cloth, a few stones and silks for scenery, a lit candle. Children sit close and quiet. The whole arrangement teaches, without a word, that what is about to happen is worth attending to. This is one of the deep gifts of the form — that it makes reverence visible and natural.

Made by human hands

Almost every puppet a Waldorf child meets is hand-made — by the teacher, by a parent, or eventually by the child themselves. Nothing about a felt mother, a wooden gnome on a stand, or a silk-headed marionette is mass-produced or plastic. The child senses, without being told, that these little beings come from the same world they themselves are growing into: a world where things are made, mended, and loved.

Steiner's own indication

Among the things Steiner said of puppetry, one phrase has been carried by generations of Waldorf teachers: that puppet-play is, in its quiet way, a balm for what modern life takes from children. The flicker, noise, and speed of contemporary media are met by their gentle opposite — a small stage, a slow gesture, a sung verse, a story that ends quietly with everyone safe again.

II.The Kinds of Puppets

Waldorf puppetry uses several distinct kinds of figure, each suited to a different age, story, and mood. The progression goes from the simplest forms in early childhood toward the more articulated marionette in the upper grades.

Finger puppets

Tiny wool-felt figures that slip over a finger or thumb. Often the very first puppets a young child encounters — gentle, intimate, made for one-on-one storytelling at home or in the kindergarten.

Standing or table puppets

Small wool figures with a flat or weighted base that stand on a tabletop landscape. The teacher moves them by hand along a "playscape" of silks, stones, and natural objects. The signature form of the Waldorf kindergarten.

Lap puppets & silk marionettes

Soft figures made with a silk head and flowing body, suspended from a few simple strings. Many Waldorf kindergarten teachers tell stories with a silk on their lap as a stage and a single lightly-strung silk marionette as the main character.

Hand puppets

Worn over the hand, with the puppeteer's fingers moving head and arms. Introduced in the early grades, often hand-sewn by the children themselves in Class 3 handwork lessons and used in class puppet plays in Class 4.

Shadow puppets

Flat figures cut from card or paper, played behind a back-lit translucent screen. Used in the middle grades, sometimes tied to a physics block on optics, and capable of moving, jointed forms — flickering tongues, flailing limbs, sinuous tails.

Marionettes

Fully-strung puppets suspended from a control bar above. Demanding to make and to operate. In many Waldorf schools, marionettes are introduced as a serious project in Class 7 — and in some, a marionette production becomes a signature performance in Class 9 or beyond.

III.Puppetry Through the Grades

As with handwork and the other arts, puppetry meets the child differently at each age — first as something received from a trusted adult, then as something the child makes and finally, in adolescence, as a craft and a performance in its own right.

~ kindergarten · ages 3–6 ~

The teacher's puppet play

standing puppets · silk marionettes · seasonal tales · puppet plays as part of the weekly rhythm

The young child receives puppet stories rather than making them. The teacher quietly sets up a small table-stage with silks, stones, and a few hand-made figures, and tells a seasonal fairy tale while the children sit close. The same story is often told for a week or more, becoming familiar, almost ritual. This is the heart of Waldorf puppetry.

~ Class 1–2 · age 6–8 ~

Watching, listening, simple play

teacher-led puppet plays · children begin to play with simple puppets in free time

Puppet plays continue to be performed for the children, often connected to the year's stories — fairy tales in Class 1, fables and saint legends in Class 2. The children gradually begin to take simple puppets into their own play, telling small stories to one another with what their teacher has set out.

~ Class 3 · age 8–9 ~

Making the first puppets

sewing hand-puppets in handwork class · simple costumes and faces

In Class 3, as part of the handwork curriculum, children typically sew their own hand-puppets — a real, useful, finished thing made with their own hands. The fingers, head, and simple costume come together, often with help from the handwork teacher, into a character the child will know well.

~ Class 4 · age 9–10 ~

The class puppet play

a whole-class performance using the hand-puppets they have made

Often in Class 4 — sometimes earlier or later — the children put on a full puppet play together, using the hand-puppets they sewed the year before. They learn lines, rehearse the movements, build a simple stage, and perform for parents and other classes. The work is collective and joyful.

~ Class 6–7 · age 11–13 ~

Marionettes and more complex projects

designing and building marionettes · jointed and articulated puppets · shadow plays

By Class 7 — sometimes 6 — students take on the marionette as a serious project. This is wonderfully matched to early adolescence: a marionette has its own anatomy, its own centre of balance, its own laws of movement, and learning to make and operate one connects to the physical and mechanical changes the students themselves are passing through. Shadow puppetry, tied to a physics block on optics, often appears in this period as well.

~ high school · age 14+ ~

The full marionette production

designing, sculpting, and operating fully-strung marionettes · writing and staging an original play

In some Waldorf high schools, Class 9 or 10 students undertake a full marionette production — sculpting heads, building bodies, assembling control bars, writing and directing the play, and performing it for the school. It is a culminating piece of work that draws on biology, handwork, drama, and writing all at once, and it lives in students' memory long after.

V.How to Make Your Own

Making your own puppet is itself a piece of Waldorf practice — slow, considered, done with natural materials, and producing a figure you will tell stories with for years. Here is what you need to know to begin.

The simplest starting point

The easiest Waldorf puppet to make is a standing wool-felt table puppet. The body is a simple felt cylinder, the head a small ball of wool wrapped in cotton knit and tied off, and the cape and hair give the figure its character. No moving parts, no strings, no stage — just a soft figure that stands on the table where the story is being told.

From there, the progression is usually:

  • 1. Finger puppets Wool felt cut to a simple shape and stitched at the sides, with a tiny embroidered or felted face. The fastest "first puppet" project.
  • 2. Standing table puppets A felt cylinder body, a small wool head wrapped in cotton knit, a cape. The classic kindergarten figure.
  • 3. Silk marionettes A wool head wrapped in silk, with a flowing silk body and a few simple strings attached to a wooden cross. Lighter, gentler, and easier than a full wooden marionette.
  • 4. Hand puppets A glove-like body that fits over the hand, with a separate head. The most involved hand-sewn project, often done as a Class 3 handwork piece.

Materials to gather

For Waldorf puppets, the materials matter. The classic palette is pure wool felt for bodies and capes, carded wool fleece for stuffing heads, cotton knit for the head's "skin," silk in plant-dyed colours for cloaks and marionette bodies, and mohair or boucle yarn for hair. Avoid synthetics — they don't age, don't soften, and don't carry warmth.

Patterns and books

  • Wool Felt Waldorf Table Puppet Pattern (Ronna McEldowney) A classic 9-page pattern for three sizes of wool-felt standing puppets, in use by kindergarten teachers for decades. Available from A Child's Dream. achildsdream.com
  • Christel Dhom — Making Magical Fairy-Tale Puppets A beloved guide to making the table puppets, silk marionettes, and small figures used in Waldorf storytelling. Step-by-step, beautifully illustrated.
  • Freya Jaffke — Toymaking with Children Includes detailed instructions for simple silk marionettes and small marionettes for children to make and use. A core text in the Waldorf craft library.
  • Suzanne Down — Juniper Tree Puppets A Waldorf puppeteer who teaches courses, sells patterns, and offers detailed online instruction in needle-felted Waldorf puppets. Excellent for anyone wanting to go deeper.
  • Maricristin Sealey — Kinder Dolls: A Waldorf Doll-Making Handbook Focused on dolls but with techniques (heads, cotton-knit skin, hair) that translate directly into puppet-making.
  • "Plays for Puppets" A small classic booklet of typical Waldorf puppet plays — many with simple songs and verses to sing as the story unfolds.

A note on the stage

You don't need a built theatre. A small wooden table, a silk draped behind for sky or forest, a few smooth stones and wooden trees, perhaps a candle lit at the side — this is the traditional Waldorf "playscape." Many kindergarten teachers simply use their lap, a silk, and one or two figures. The simpler the staging, the more the imagination of the watching child has to do.

The puppet moves only a little. The story moves only a little. And the child, watching, moves a great deal inside.

VI.Materials & Suppliers — A Buying Guide

For homeschooling parents, Waldorf teachers, and adult crafters wanting to make their own puppets, here are the specific materials — with weights, sizes, and the suppliers who carry them. The Waldorf puppet palette is small: wool, silk, cotton, mohair, a few simple tools. But the quality of each piece matters, and finding the right materials is half the battle.

A basic puppet-making kit

  • Head stuffing: carded wool fleece (sometimes called "wool batting" or "wool roving"), 50–100g for a small puppet
  • Skin: cotton tubular gauze (1 inch) or thrifted cotton knit (a soft t-shirt works)
  • Body: pure wool felt, 1mm or 3mm thickness, in soft natural colours
  • Cloak/marionette body: pure silk in plant-dyed colours, around 8mm or 11mm habotai weight
  • Hair: mohair locks, bouclé yarn, or fine merino yarn
  • Stitching: strong cotton thread, embroidery floss for features
  • For marionettes: two thin wooden dowels (about 10 inches), fishing line or doll string, small wooden beads

The materials, one by one

Wool fleece for stuffing

The head of every Waldorf puppet is a tight, firm ball of wool fleece — wrapped, shaped, and tied. The wool gives the head its warmth, its slight weight, and its lasting quality.

  • Type: carded wool batting or wool roving — long-fibre, unspun, dyed or undyed
  • Quantity: 50–100g for a small standing puppet; 150–250g for a larger marionette or doll-sized puppet
  • Where to buy: A Child's Dream, Acorns & Twigs, Bella Luna Toys in the US; Myriad Natural Toys in the UK; West Earl Woolen Mill in Pennsylvania (recommended by Waldorf puppet-makers for fair prices and fast shipping)

Cotton tubular gauze or knit (the "skin")

The wool head is covered in a layer of cotton knit, which acts as the puppet's "skin" — smooth, soft, and able to take stitched eyes and mouth.

  • Tubular cotton gauze: 1-inch diameter (sold by the metre); the easiest option since it slides over the head as a tube and ties off at the neck. A Child's Dream stocks the Waldorf-standard tubular gauze
  • Doll tricot (for larger puppets): a stretchier cotton-knit fabric sold by the half-metre; Bella Luna Toys and Magic Cabin carry it
  • Thrifted t-shirt: a soft, fine-knit cotton t-shirt (no synthetic blend) works beautifully and is what many home puppet-makers actually use — cut a rectangle, gather around the head, tie off at the neck

Wool felt (for bodies, capes, and small puppets)

For finger puppets and the small standing table puppets used in Waldorf kindergartens, pure wool felt is the material of choice.

  • Type: 100% wool felt (not wool-blend, not synthetic). Wool-blend felts contain polyester and don't age or feel the same way
  • Thickness: 1mm (3 oz) for finger puppets and small details; 3mm (5 oz) for sturdier standing-puppet bodies
  • Colours: the Waldorf palette favours soft, plant-dye-like colours — apricot, rose, deep forest green, dusty blue, ochre, soft browns. Avoid bright primary felt
  • Where to buy: A Child's Dream (the most extensive Waldorf felt selection); Acorns & Twigs; Filges in Germany (the canonical European source); The Felt Pod and Wool Felt Central for serious crafters

Silk (for cloaks and marionette bodies)

The silk marionette and the silk-cloaked standing puppet are signature Waldorf forms. The silk gives the puppet its airy, otherworldly quality — light catches it, it floats as the puppet moves, and the plant-dyed colours have a depth synthetic fabric can't match.

  • Habotai silk: the standard silk for puppet bodies and cloaks. 8mm (light, flowing) for cloaks and marionette bodies; 11mm (slightly heavier) for larger pieces
  • Pre-cut silk handkerchiefs: 17 × 17 inches is the most useful size for a single small marionette; 22 × 22 inches for larger pieces
  • Colour: Waldorf families typically dye their own silks using plant dyes or natural dyes (turmeric, madder, indigo, onion skin) for the most beautiful, soft tones. Pre-dyed silks are also widely available
  • Where to buy: Dharma Trading Company (dharmatrading.com) — the most widely recommended source by Waldorf puppet-makers worldwide. They sell undyed habotai silks at fair prices, plus the dyes and instructions to colour them. Sarah's Silks (sarahssilks.com) sells ready-dyed Waldorf-quality silks; A Child's Dream carries plant-dyed silks; Beautiful Silks in Australia and Whaleys Bradford in the UK are regional alternatives

Mohair, bouclé, and yarn (for hair)

The hair of a Waldorf puppet is often the most expressive part — and the part that makes a soft figure feel suddenly alive.

  • Mohair locks: unspun locks of mohair from young goats; gentle, curly, with a natural shine. Used for the most beautiful Waldorf doll and puppet hair. Plant-dyed or natural
  • Bouclé yarn: a looped, curly yarn that mimics hair texture without the cost of mohair locks. A practical middle option
  • Fine merino yarn: for braids, long straight hair, or short stitched hair styles
  • Where to buy: A Child's Dream (Waldorf-specific mohair locks); Bella Luna Toys; Magic Cabin for doll hair; Living Felt for naturally dyed mohair

Marionette hardware

For silk marionettes — the gentler, lighter alternative to traditional wooden marionettes — you need very little.

  • Two wooden dowels: about 10 inches (25cm) long, ¼ inch (6mm) diameter, joined in a cross. Found sticks from the garden work beautifully if smooth
  • Fishing line or doll-making thread: strong, almost invisible — about 2 metres total per puppet
  • Glue or yarn: for binding the cross-stick at its centre
  • Small wooden beads (optional): for weighting hands or feet so they swing naturally

Stitching tools

  • Embroidery needles: size 22 chenille or tapestry needles for facial features and yarn hair; sharps size 5–7 for fine stitching
  • Strong doll-making thread: for tying off necks and joining limbs (often a heavier waxed or button-and-craft thread)
  • Embroidery floss: DMC or Anchor stranded cotton, in soft natural colours, for eyes and mouths
  • Small sharp scissors
