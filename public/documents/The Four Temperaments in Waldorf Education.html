Waldorf teaching notes

The Four Temperaments

What Steiner told the first Waldorf teachers about sanguine, choleric, melancholic and phlegmatic children, and how to use it

Steiner took the old idea of four temperaments and made it a working tool for teachers. In August 1919, a month before the first Waldorf school opened in Stuttgart, he sat down with the teachers and told them that recognizing these four types was one of the most important things they would do.

1.Where the idea sits

He described temperament as the place where two things meet: what we inherit from our family line and what we bring with us as an individual. Most people are a mixture, with one temperament usually leading.

2.What lies behind each one

When he spoke to the teachers about children, Steiner tied each temperament to the part of the child that is strongest at that point. He said that in adults the picture shifts a little, so this is a way of looking at children in particular.

TemperamentWhat leads in the child
MelancholicThe I (the individual self) is very strong from the earliest years
CholericThe astral body, the life of feeling and drive
SanguineThe etheric body, the life forces
PhlegmaticThe physical body

He added a warning about the first one. People tend to treat a melancholic child as somehow favored. That is an easy mistake to make, and he asked teachers not to make it.

3.The four children

Sanguine

interested in everything, for a minute

What you see

A child who takes up many things, but only briefly. Interest comes quickly and goes just as fast. Attention is easy to catch and hard to hold.

What helps

Give them a quick succession of vivid, colorful pictures, ideas, events and facts. Don't try to drive the quickness out of them. Watch closely to see what actually catches their attention, because that is where you can hold them. Steiner also said the teacher should be serious with these children and give strong, clear pictures that stay in the mind.

Choleric

will out in front

What you see

A child who puts their will forward loudly. Steiner's word was blustering. Response is strong and easily set off.

What helps

Find them a hard, demanding task, mental or physical, that uses up their energy and drive. Don't try to stop the raging. When the temper comes, the teacher stays calm outside and cool inside.

Melancholic

busy on the inside

What you see

A child who turns inward and broods. It is hard to get an impression of the outer world through to them, yet they are far from idle. Something is going on within.

What helps

Help them out of self-pity and self-absorption, and keep them from going over and over what the class has already left behind. Wake their interest in what is around them. Steiner said the teacher should act a little sanguine and describe the world in a lively way. He read the brooding as an inner reaching toward spirit that runs up against a heavy body.

Phlegmatic

quiet inside and out

What you see

A child who is not active inwardly and shows little interest in the outer world either. Response is weak and hard to rouse.

What helps

Here the teacher splits in two. Inside, feel real sympathy and watch for every small sign of life. Outside, stay fairly indifferent, so the child finds a mirror in you. Steiner said that if you can get through the apathy, this child turns out to be very interesting.

4.Meet it, don't fight it

This is the center of Steiner's advice. The worst way to help a temperament, he said, is to build up its opposite. Training a sanguine child by suppressing the sanguine qualities does harm. You go toward the temperament and work with it. Over time the temperament loosens its grip and begins to balance with the others.

His example was a choleric child who throws a jar of paint on the floor. At that moment you stay completely calm and say something short, like "Look, you threw the paint jar." The next day, when the child has settled, you talk it over with sympathy. The child goes through the whole scene again in memory, and you both judge it quietly. Steiner said there is no other way to help a child master a temper.

5.In the classroom

Steiner told the teachers to spend the first months watching, and to rearrange the seating gradually into four groups, one per temperament. In the first school, with boys and girls, that meant eight groups.

The reason is practical. When you show the class something to look at, you turn toward the sanguine group. When you want the children to think about it or form a view, you turn toward the melancholic group. Each group gives the other what it lacks. He said this should become habit, a feeling for where to turn, because if you do it by rule you lose your spontaneity.

Children of the same temperament sitting together do not make each other more extreme. They tone each other down. Cholerics seated together chatter less than they would among other children.

He gave the teachers a simple diagram with two questions. How strong is the response? How easily is attention caught? Cholerics have the most strength and the most easily aroused attention. Sanguines are easily aroused, but there is little strength behind it. Melancholics are not easily roused, yet when something does take hold, the quality of attention is strong. Phlegmatics have the least of both: the least strength and the attention that is hardest to arouse. Neighboring types blend into each other, and sanguine and phlegmatic are often found together. Phlegmatic and choleric are as far apart as the two poles, and so are melancholic and sanguine. So he suggested seating the phlegmatics together, the cholerics on the opposite side, and the melancholics and sanguines in between.

SanguineAttention is easily aroused, but there is little strength behind itA quick succession of dramatic, colorful pictures, ideas, events and facts. The teacher watches closely to see what catches the child's attention.
CholericThe greatest amount of attention and strength, and the most easily arousedA hard, demanding mental or physical task to use up their energy and drive.

6.Colors and moods

Waldorf teaching takes its lead on color from Goethe's Theory of Colours, especially the part called the Didactic Section, where he describes what each color does to our feelings. In school the idea is that children should live through those effects themselves. They paint with thin, wet watercolors on damp paper, where nothing can be right or wrong. Steiner said these experiences make a child's inner pictures more mobile, the feelings more supple and the will more flexible. He also preferred Goethe's way of looking at color to the physicists' version, and said so plainly in a 1923 lecture.

Goethe divided the colors into two sides. Yellow, and the reds that lean toward yellow, are the warm, active side: lively and pushing outward. Blue, and the colors that lean toward blue, are the cool side: quiet, yielding and drawing inward. Green sits between them as rest. A color works like a mood you can see, and that is why it fits so well with the temperaments.

TemperamentColors often paired with itThe mood
CholericRed, orange, strong warm colorsActive and pushing outward, like the will itself
SanguineYellow, light and bright colors, the rainbowLight, lively, always changing
MelancholicBlue, violet, deep cool colorsInward, quiet, serious
PhlegmaticGreen, soft blue-greenRestful and steady

These pairings are common Waldorf practice. I did not find a fixed color chart from Steiner himself, so take them as a starting point and not a rule.

What Steiner did stress is that the colors do the work, not the teacher. The teacher tells color stories and lets the children feel their way in. The weekly painting day also gives the teacher a closer look into each child's inner life. Watch what a child reaches for, what they avoid, and how they paint when nobody is telling them what to do. In his 1919 talks he also urged teachers to look for forms that help a child whose temperament is too one-sided, and in the eighth grade the class sculpts the four temperaments as figures.

Colour

Rudolf Steiner, CW 291

The book to read if this section caught your interest. It gathers three lectures given in Dornach in May 1921 and nine shorter talks from 1914 to 1924. Steiner builds on Goethe's color theory and shows how color works on our feelings, our health and our sense of well-being. He also separates what he calls image colors from luster colors, and from that he works out a practical way of painting. It is the fullest collection of his thoughts on color, painting and art that exists in English.

7.What it asks of the adult

Steiner kept coming back to one point. A great deal passes from the soul of the teacher to the soul of the child, and much of it is invisible. When you stay calm beside an angry child, or feel real warmth for a sluggish one, you are educating through what you are. He told the teachers not to rush their preparation but to think these things through until they became their own.

None of this is limited to school. A parent can do the same thing at the kitchen table: watch first, meet the child where they are, and keep their own mood steady.

8.A few cautions

The four types are a way of looking, and no child fits one perfectly. Steiner himself said the temperament wears down its sharp edges over time, and that by about the tenth year the differences are largely smoothed out. Use the temperaments to understand a child better. Don't use them as a label.
