Theosophy

Rudolf Steiner · GA 9, 1904

The easiest way in. It lays out the human being as physical body, etheric body, astral body and ego, then covers reincarnation, karma and what happens to the soul after death. It is short and well organized. If you read only one book to find out whether Steiner is for you, make it this one. There is a full study section on it further on.

His clearest account of how we know things and how we act freely. It is the most philosophical of the core books, and the one the rest of his work rests on. Michael Wilson’s translation (Rudolf Steiner Press) is widely recommended. There is a full study section on it further on.

The nearest thing to a full statement of his teaching: the make-up of the human being, the stages of the earth’s evolution, karma and reincarnation in depth, and the path of inner development. V.A suggested order

One way through, if you want a plan.

  1. Theosophy, to get the overall picture.
  2. The Philosophy of Freedom, slowly, over a few months.
  3. How to Know Higher Worlds, if you want the practical side.
  4. The Education of the Child, then The Study of Man, if education is what brought you here.
  5. An Outline of Esoteric Science, once you are comfortable with Steiner’s language.

You do not have to follow this. Plenty of people start with the Waldorf books because that is how they met anthroposophy, and plenty never read the philosophical ones at all. Pick the one that matches what you are curious about.

VII.Study guide: The Philosophy of Freedom

What it is. Published in 1894 and revised by Steiner in 1918. He considered it his central book and said that everything he later wrote could be traced back to it. It was written before he had any spiritual teaching to present, and it reads as philosophy. It makes no appeal to clairvoyance or to belief.

The question. Can a person act freely, or is every action the result of causes we do not control? To answer it Steiner first has to ask what knowing is. So the book starts with how we know things and ends with how we should act.

The shape of the book. There are two parts. The first, “The Science of Freedom,” deals with knowledge: what we mean by thinking, the difference between what we perceive and what we think about it, and whether there are limits to knowing. The second, “The Reality of Freedom,” turns to action: what moves us to act, what a free act would be, and how this fits with the rest of life.

The main ideas.

  • Percept and concept. The world first comes to us as raw perception. Thinking supplies the concepts that make sense of it. Knowledge happens where the two meet.
  • Thinking as the one thing we see from the inside. Everything else we know about, we know at a distance. Our own thinking we watch while we do it. For Steiner that makes it the right starting point.
  • Motives of action. We can act out of habit, from duty, because we were told to, or from a moral principle we have adopted. Steiner argues that the freest action comes from moral intuition: seeing what a particular situation asks for, and doing it out of love for the deed.
  • Moral imagination. In the second part he explains how a person comes up with the ideas to act on, and why a free person cannot just apply a rule.
  • Ethical individualism. Moral life belongs to individuals, who take responsibility for what they do. It is not simply handed down.

How to read it.

  • Read the first part slowly. It is the hardest, and it decides whether the rest is convincing. Chapters 3 and 4 (on thinking, and on percept and concept) are where most readers either get hold of the argument or lose it.
  • Argue with it. Steiner wants you to test it against your own experience, not accept it. If a step seems wrong, write down why.
  • Do the small experiments he suggests. Watch yourself think about a simple object, and notice the moment a concept joins a perception.
  • Expect to go back. Most people say it clicks on the second or third reading.
  • A reading group helps more here than with any other Steiner book, because the book is an argument and arguments go better with someone to answer.

A rough pace. If you read alone, one chapter a week is manageable and leaves time to reread. Fourteen chapters plus the closing section takes about four months.

Questions to bring to it.

  • When I “think about” something, what am I actually doing, and can I observe it?
  • Is there anything in my own recent actions that I would call truly free? What would have to be true for it to count?
  • How does acting from “love for the deed” differ from acting out of duty?
  • Where do I find Steiner unconvincing, and is that a problem with his argument or with how I read it?

Translations. The Michael Wilson translation (Rudolf Steiner Press) is the one most often recommended and uses the title The Philosophy of Freedom. Michael Lipson’s translation, Intuitive Thinking as a Spiritual Path (SteinerBooks), is also widely used, and Rita Stebbing’s is titled The Philosophy of Spiritual Activity. It is worth reading a few pages of two translations and picking the one you can follow. The book is free at rsarchive.org.

Companion reading. Truth and Science (GA 3) covers the same ground on knowledge in a more academic style. Steiner’s earlier work on Goethe’s theory of knowledge shows where the ideas came from. Once you have finished, Theosophy and How to Know Higher Worlds read differently, since they carry on from the position this book establishes.

VIII.Study guide: Theosophy

What it is. Published in 1904, and the first of Steiner’s books to set out his picture of the human being and the spiritual world in one place. It is GA 9, written for general readers, and he revised it several times. The word is used in its literal sense, wisdom about the divine. It is not a summary of the Theosophical Society’s teaching. Steiner had worked inside that society and parted ways with it around 1912–13, when he founded the Anthroposophical Society.

The shape of the book. After an introduction there are four chapters.

  • The essential nature of the human being. Body, soul and spirit, and the parts of each: the physical body, the life or etheric body, the soul body, then the three soul members, and then the three spirit members.
  • Reincarnation and destiny. How the spirit returns to earth life again and again, and how what a person does in one life shapes the next. This is where karma comes in.
  • The three worlds. The soul world, the spirit world and the physical world, and what the soul passes through after death.
  • The path of knowledge. A short chapter on how a person could come to see these things for themselves. It leads straight into How to Know Higher Worlds.

The main ideas.

  1. A human being is not only a body. Steiner describes a physical body, a life body, a soul body and an “I,” and says each has its own laws.
  2. The “I” is the part that carries on from life to life, and it is the part that can learn.
  3. Life after death has stages of its own, and what happens there is connected to how the person lived.
  4. Destiny is not luck. It comes from earlier lives, and what a person does now sets up later ones.
  5. None of this is meant to be taken on faith. Steiner says the claims can be checked by anyone willing to do the inner work.

How to read it.

  • Read the introduction first. It explains why Steiner thinks a book like this can be written at all.
  • Go through the first chapter in small pieces. The terms are new, so keep a list of the parts of the human being and what each one does.
  • Read it as a description to test against your own experience, not as a creed. That is how Steiner asked to be read.
  • Finish the whole book once before you go back and study any single chapter.
  • Reread the first chapter at the end. It reads differently once you know where the book is heading.

A rough pace. It is short. One chapter a week, with the introduction in the first week, takes about five weeks. Many readers go slower, and that is fine.

Questions to bring to it.

  • What does Steiner mean by the “I,” and how is it different from my personality or my mood?
  • Which parts of the description fit something I have seen in myself, and which ask me to take things on trust?
  • If destiny comes from earlier lives, how much room is left for freedom? (This is the question The Philosophy of Freedom takes up.)
  • What would it take for me to check any of this myself?

Translations. Catherine Creeger’s translation (SteinerBooks) is a common choice, and the book is free at rsarchive.org. Titles and subtitles vary between editions, so check what you are buying.

Companion reading. How to Know Higher Worlds picks up where the last chapter leaves off. An Outline of Esoteric Science covers the same ground at much greater length. Theosophy of the Rosicrucian is a lecture course on the same themes from 1907. The Philosophy of Freedom is the book to read if the chapter on destiny leaves you asking about freedom.
