I.Why Festivals Matter

In Waldorf education, the festivals of the year are not pleasant extras. They are the very heartbeat of the school — the way the cycle of the seasons enters the life of the child, year after year, until it becomes part of how they understand the world.

Waldorf schools see the human being and the earth as deeply connected. As the year breathes out into summer and back in toward winter, so the child too breathes with it — outward into activity and light in the warm half of the year, inward into quiet and inner work in the dark half. The festivals mark and honour these turnings. They give the child something steady and beautiful to return to, year after year, in a world where time can otherwise feel formless.

The seasons made meaningful

A child who walks the same lantern walk every November, dances the same Maypole every May, and meets the dragon at Michaelmas year after year is given something quietly precious: a sense that the year has a shape. The festivals draw an arc across each twelve months and make of them not a stretch of identical days but a meaningful journey.

Inner gestures, not just outer events

Each festival in the Waldorf calendar carries an inner mood as well as an outer celebration. Michaelmas asks for courage. Martinmas asks for compassion. The Winter Spiral asks for quiet inner light. May Day asks for joy and renewal. These are not lessons spoken to the children but qualities lived through the songs, the stories, the foods, and the activities of the day — taken in through the whole being and stored, slowly, in the soul.

Community made visible

Festivals also gather the school community — children, teachers, and parents — into shared celebration. The work of preparing lanterns, baking dragon bread, weaving Maypole crowns, or carrying candles through a spiral is done together, and the gathering itself is part of the festival's meaning. Generations of Waldorf families remember the autumn lantern walk or the spring May Faire with a particular tenderness, because community itself was woven into the experience.

A note on origins

Waldorf education was born in early-twentieth-century Europe, and its festival cycle reflects that origin — drawing on the older Christian calendar (Michaelmas, Martinmas, Advent), on pre-Christian European nature festivals (May Day, midsummer, the solstices), and on Steiner's particular spiritual reading of the year. Schools today honour the diversity of their families and often add festivals from other traditions (Diwali, Hanukkah, Lunar New Year), but the core Waldorf festivals retain their distinctive shape and meaning.

II.A Note on the Two Hemispheres

Before we walk through the festivals one by one, a question that matters especially for Waldorf families in South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, Argentina, Chile, and the rest of the southern world: when do these festivals actually fall?

Waldorf festivals are, at their root, festivals of the seasons. Michaelmas is the festival of the autumn equinox — the moment the year tips from light toward dark. Midsummer is the festival of the summer solstice — the year's fullest expansion. The Advent Spiral belongs to the winter solstice — the deepest inward moment of the year. The European calendar dates that have come down through Waldorf tradition (29 September for Michaelmas, 21 June for midsummer) are not the festivals themselves; they are the dates on which the northern hemisphere meets those seasonal moments.

In the southern hemisphere, the seasons are reversed. The autumn equinox falls in late March; the winter solstice in June; the spring equinox in September; the summer solstice in December. A southern Waldorf school faithful to the festival cycle therefore celebrates Michaelmas in late March, the Advent Spiral in June, May Day in October or November, and Midsummer in December.

The festivals follow the seasons, not the calendar This is the most important principle to grasp. A Cape Town family does not celebrate Michaelmas in September pretending it is autumn. They celebrate Michaelmas at their own autumn — in late March, when the days are genuinely shortening, the leaves are genuinely turning, and the gesture of inner gathering and courage actually meets the world outside. The Waldorf festival year, lived honestly in the southern hemisphere, simply runs six months out of phase with the European calendar.

The two patterns at a glance

Here is how the major Waldorf festivals fall in each hemisphere, by season:

  • Autumn equinox — Michaelmas: late September (north) / late March (south)
  • Deep autumn — Martinmas & Lantern Walk: 11 November (north) / mid-May (south)
  • Winter solstice — Advent Spiral, Winter Fair: December (north) / June (south)
  • Spring equinox — Easter: March/April (north) / September/October (south)
  • Spring festival — May Day, Maypole: 1 May (north) / 1 November or thereabouts (south)
  • Summer solstice — Midsummer, St John's: 21–24 June (north) / 21–24 December (south)

A word about Christmas and Easter

The two great fixed-date festivals — Christmas (25 December) and Easter (the first Sunday after the full moon following the spring equinox) — present a particular challenge in the southern hemisphere. Christmas falls at the southern summer solstice, not the winter solstice. Easter falls at the southern autumn equinox, not the spring equinox.

Some southern Waldorf schools and families keep the European dates and celebrate Christmas in summer (with mince pies eaten outdoors and beach picnics), while quietly marking the actual winter solstice with their own Advent Spiral in June. Others, like Michael Mount Waldorf School in Johannesburg, have made deliberate choices about which festivals to align to the European calendar and which to align to the southern seasons — there is no single "right" answer, and different schools have made different thoughtful choices.

How this guide is organised

Each festival section below gives:

  • Its underlying meaning, which is the same in either hemisphere
  • The traditional northern hemisphere calendar date
  • The corresponding southern hemisphere seasonal date
  • Notes on how southern schools and families typically adapt the celebration where this is helpful

III.Michaelmas — the festival of courage

Northern hemisphere around 29 September, at the autumn equinox
Southern hemisphere late March, at the southern autumn equinox

Michaelmas is the first festival of the Waldorf school year. As summer ends and the days begin to shorten, it asks the question that will carry the children through the dark half of the year: where will I find my courage?

The festival is named for the Archangel Michael, who in Christian, Jewish, and Islamic tradition is a figure of courage, goodness, and the triumph of light over darkness. In the legend most often told in Waldorf schools, Michael — sometimes alongside Saint George — meets a great dragon and, rather than killing it, tames it through courage, strength, and the inner light he carries. The dragon is not simply evil to be destroyed; it is the wild, destructive force in the world and in ourselves that must be met and transformed.

Why it falls at the autumn equinox

Michaelmas sits close to the autumn equinox, when light and dark are equal and the year is just beginning to tip toward winter. The harvest is being gathered in. The warmth of summer is fading. The festival meets this moment with a clear inner gesture: gather your courage and your inner strength to face the dark months ahead. The outer light is waning; the inner light must begin to be kindled.

In Europe and North America, this is late September. In South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, and the rest of the southern hemisphere, the autumn equinox falls in late March — and southern Waldorf schools faithful to the seasonal meaning of the festival celebrate Michaelmas then. Willunga Waldorf School in South Australia describes their March Michaelmas precisely this way: as the days grow shorter and summer gives way to autumn, "it is time to look inward and find one's own strength and courage." The festival is the same; the date is six months apart.

What the dragon means

For the children, the dragon is not just a creature in a story. It is also the dragon inside each of them — fear, anger, laziness, unkindness, whatever holds them back from doing what they know is right. Michaelmas tells them, in pictures rather than lectures, that this dragon can be faced and even befriended. The festival plants the image of a courageous Self who meets darkness with light.

Brave and true I will be,
Each good deed sets me free.
Each kind word makes me strong.
I will fight for the right;
I will conquer the wrong. — a traditional Michaelmas verse spoken in Waldorf classrooms
How it is celebrated
  • A whole-school pageant of Saint Michael and the dragon, performed by the older grades — the dragon often made by several children inside a long fabric costume
  • Children dressed in red, the colour of courage
  • Games of strength, balance, and bravery — obstacle courses, tug-of-war, tests of skill
  • Baking and sharing dragon bread — a loaf shaped like a curled dragon, sometimes with currants for scales and almond claws
  • Wooden swords and golden stars
  • Songs of Saint Michael and verses of courage spoken in morning circle for the whole Michaelmas season

IV.Martinmas & the Lantern Walk — the festival of compassion

Northern hemisphere 11 November, deep autumn dusk
Southern hemisphere mid-May, as southern autumn deepens

As the days darken and winter approaches, Martinmas asks: can you carry a small light into the cold and dark, both for yourself and for others?

The festival honours Saint Martin of Tours, a Roman soldier who, on a cold winter's night, met a half-frozen beggar by the city gate. The other soldiers passed by; Martin took the cloak from his own shoulders, cut it in two with his sword, and wrapped half around the freezing man. He went on to become known as the patron saint of beggars and outcasts — a figure of gentleness, humility, and the warmth one human being can give another.

Why the lantern

Martinmas falls in the deep autumn, between the fiery out-breath of Michaelmas and the deep in-breath of the winter holidays. The light kindled at Michaelmas — that small inner courage — now needs protecting, like a candle in a cold wind. The handmade lantern becomes the symbol: a small, fragile light that the child carries safely through the dark, sharing its warmth with whoever walks beside them.

The walk itself

The Martinmas lantern walk is one of the most beloved and atmospheric of all Waldorf festivals. At dusk, the children gather with their handmade paper lanterns, each one carefully crafted in the days before. The candles are lit, the lanterns glow softly, and the children — usually the youngest, from kindergarten through about Class 3 — walk in a slow procession through the school grounds, a meadow, or a wooded path. They sing lantern songs as they go, and parents walk beside them in silence. No flashlights, no phones, no rushing. Just small lights, soft singing, and the cold autumn dusk.

I go with my little lantern,
My lantern goes with me.
Above the stars are shining,
Below shine you and me.
The cock does crow, the cat says mew,
Rabimmel, rabammel, rabum. — a traditional German lantern song

The inner gesture

What the children learn through the lantern walk is something no lesson could teach: that even the smallest light brings relief to the darkness, and that walking together with our lights makes the dark bearable. The story of Saint Martin and his cut cloak is the wider image: kindness given freely to someone who has less, asking nothing back. Many Waldorf schools also mark Martinmas with food and clothing drives, turning the inner gesture into a real act in the world.

How it is celebrated
  • Children make their own paper, glass-jar, or tin lanterns in the days leading up to the festival
  • The story of Saint Martin and the beggar is told in the classrooms
  • At dusk, families gather, lanterns are lit, and a slow procession begins
  • Soft lantern songs are sung as the children walk; the mood is quiet and reverent
  • Some schools include a small enactment of the Saint Martin story
  • Food drives, clothing drives, or other acts of giving connect the festival to real-world compassion

V.The Advent Spiral & Winter Solstice — the festival of inner light

Northern hemisphere early to mid December, near the winter solstice
Southern hemisphere June, at the southern winter solstice

In the darkest weeks of the year, Waldorf schools across the world hold a quiet, profound ceremony: each child walks alone through a spiral of evergreen boughs to a single candle at the centre, lights their own small candle, and carries it back out into the world.

This is the Advent Spiral — also called the Winter Spiral or the Spiral of Light — and it is held in the weeks leading up to the winter solstice, when the days are at their shortest and the nights at their longest. It belongs to early childhood and the lower grades, and it is one of the most moving experiences of the Waldorf year.

The spiral itself

The room is dimmed. On the floor, a spiral path is laid out in fresh evergreen boughs — pine, fir, cedar — perhaps two or three turns from the outside in. At the centre sits a single large candle, already burning. Around the spiral are placed natural objects: stones, pinecones, golden stars, sometimes small wooden animals. The children sit quietly around the edge of the room. Soft music plays — often a single instrument, a lyre or a flute. One by one, each child is called by name. They take an unlit candle, often pressed into an apple, and walk slowly inward along the spiral path. At the centre, they kneel and light their candle from the flame at the heart. Then they walk back out, choosing a place along the spiral to set their now-burning candle down. With every child, more lights appear, and the dark room grows slowly luminous.

What it means

The image is simple and very deep. As the outer world grows darkest, each person must journey inward to find a light, then carry it back out to share with others. The spiral itself is an ancient form — a turning inward and then outward again. By the end of the ceremony, the whole spiral glows with the children's candles, a path of light laid down in the darkest time of the year.

How it is celebrated
  • A spiral of evergreen boughs laid on the floor of a darkened, candle-lit room
  • A single large candle burning at the centre
  • Each child walks the spiral in turn, lights their candle, and sets it down on the path
  • Soft live music — lyre, flute, or harp — accompanies the walk
  • Natural objects placed along the path: stones, crystals, pinecones, golden stars
  • An apple is sometimes used to hold the candle, recalling the fruit of the year's harvest
  • Parents are often invited to watch in silence; the mood is one of deep reverence

Related midwinter customs

Around the same time, many Waldorf schools also celebrate Saint Nicholas Day (6 December), when children's shoes are left out overnight to be filled with nuts, fruit, and small gifts; Hanukkah in classrooms with Jewish families; and the four-week build-up of Advent itself, often marked with a weekly candle lit at home and small seasonal verses spoken each week — first the stones, then the plants, then the animals, then the human being, as the light grows.

VI.The Christkindlmarkt & Winter Fair — the festival of community and the gift made by hand

Northern hemisphere late November or December, during Advent
Southern hemisphere May or June; the southern Winter Fair sits in cooler weather as in Europe

Of all the festivals in the Waldorf year, the Christkindlmarkt — known in English-speaking schools as the Winter Fair, Advent Fair, or Holiday Fair — is the one where the entire school community gathers, opens its doors to the wider world, and shows what a year of Waldorf hand-work, music, and warmth looks like all at once.

The name comes from the German Christkindlmarkt — literally "Christ Child Market" — a tradition that goes back more than five hundred years to the late medieval markets of Dresden, Nuremberg, and Munich, where townspeople gathered in the four weeks of Advent to buy handcrafted goods, warm food, and small gifts for the coming holiday. Many Waldorf schools, with their roots in German-speaking Europe, have kept the name and the spirit. Others call it simply the Winter Fair or Holiday Fair, but the heart of the event is the same.

Why it matters in a Waldorf school

The Christkindlmarkt is unlike any of the inward, contemplative festivals around it. Where the Advent Spiral is hushed and reverent, the Winter Fair is alive — full of voices, music, the smell of cinnamon and pine, candles being dipped, puppet shows unfolding, students performing, families eating together. It is the moment when the year's quiet work — the handwork lessons, the bread-baking, the felting, the woodwork, the painting — flows out into something the whole community can see, touch, taste, and take home.

The gift made by hand

This is the central idea, and it goes deep. In a world where almost everything bought for the holidays is mass-produced, shipped from far away, and forgotten quickly, the Christkindlmarkt is a quiet act of resistance. Everything on the tables is made by hand — usually by the parents, teachers, and students of the school itself, sometimes alongside local artisans. Beeswax candles, knitted hats, felted gnomes, wooden toys, hand-sewn puppets, jars of jam, pots of honey, paper stars, woolen slippers, ceramic bowls. Each object carries the time of the person who made it, and when a child sees their parent buy a beeswax candle and place it gently in a basket, they are seeing what it means to value something because it was made slowly and with care.

A whole school comes together

Preparing for the Christkindlmarkt is a months-long act of community. Each class typically takes responsibility for one part of the event — one class runs the candle-dipping room, another bakes for the café, another stages a puppet show, another organises the silent auction, another tends the children's craft room or the magical "gnome forest" the youngest children walk through. Parents hold meetings, plan menus, sew, knit, and felt for months. By the time the fair opens, the work of preparing it has already done much of what the festival is meant to do: drawn the community together in shared, useful labour.

A doorway into Waldorf life

The Christkindlmarkt is also, for most Waldorf schools, the great open door of the year. It is held on a Saturday in late November or December and welcomes the wider public — neighbours, friends, alumni, families curious about Waldorf education. Walking into a Waldorf school transformed for the fair — evergreen boughs along the corridors, candlelight in the windows, the smell of mulled cider, children singing, a puppet show beginning in the kindergarten — is many families' first real encounter with Waldorf life. The fair is, in a quiet way, the school's most articulate self-introduction.

The Christkind, the angel of the season

In German tradition, the Christkind from whom the market takes its name is an angel-like figure — a "spirit of Christmas" usually depicted with golden hair, white robes, and wings — who in Catholic and Protestant Germany brings the children's gifts on Christmas Eve. (The custom dates to Martin Luther, who in the 1530s wanted to shift Christmas gift-giving from Saint Nicholas's day on 6 December to Christmas itself, and named the new gift-bringer the Christkind.) Many Waldorf schools incorporate this gentle figure — sometimes a senior student appears as the Christkind to open the fair with a verse, light the first candle, or hand small gifts to the youngest children.

How it is celebrated
  • A school-wide community fair held on a Saturday in late November or December
  • Handcrafted goods — beeswax candles, felted toys, wooden puppets, knitted clothing, pottery, jams, baked goods — made by parents, teachers, and students
  • Beeswax candle-dipping rooms where children make their own candles to take home
  • A puppet show staged in the kindergarten, often by the early-childhood teachers
  • A "gnome forest," "enchanted room," or "fairy garden" for the youngest children to walk through
  • Live music — student choirs, recorder groups, parent ensembles — playing throughout the day
  • A café or "tea house" serving mulled cider, gingerbread, soup, and stollen
  • A silent auction or raffle of class-made artworks and donated handcrafts
  • Outside vendors — local artisans whose work fits the Waldorf aesthetic of natural, hand-made beauty
  • Sometimes the Christkind herself appears, bringing a verse and small gifts

Why it endures

Many Waldorf schools' fairs have been running for thirty, forty, even fifty years — outliving curriculum changes, building moves, and generations of families. They endure because they answer a real human hunger that the December rush in modern life rarely satisfies: the longing for slowness, for things made with care, for small handmade gifts that mean something, for community gathered in candlelight and song. For many alumni, the Christkindlmarkt is the single most vivid memory of their Waldorf years — and many return as adults, year after year, to keep walking through that particular doorway.

VII.May Day & the Maypole — the festival of joyful spring

Northern hemisphere 1 May, the height of European spring
Southern hemisphere October or 1 November, at the height of southern spring

If Michaelmas in autumn asks for courage and the Winter Spiral asks for quiet inner light, May Day in spring asks for something else entirely: joy. Pure, dancing, ribboned, flower-crowned joy.

May Day falls midway between the spring equinox and the summer solstice, marking the full arrival of spring in the northern hemisphere. The festival is older than Waldorf — older even than Christianity in Europe. The Celts called it Beltane; the Romans honoured the goddess Flora, mistress of flowers; northern peoples celebrated it as the marriage of heaven and earth. Waldorf schools take up this ancient festival as a great whole-school celebration of warmth, light, growth, and community.

The Maypole

At the heart of the celebration is the Maypole itself: a tall wooden pole crowned with greenery and flowers, with long coloured ribbons trailing down from the top. Each dancer takes a ribbon, and as the music begins, they skip in interweaving patterns around the pole — under and over, in and out — slowly braiding the ribbons into intricate patterns down the length of the pole. Then, in the second half of the dance, the steps are reversed and the ribbons are unwoven again.

The Maypole has been called many things — the tree of life, a symbol of the sun's light entering the earth, a picture of fertility and the renewing of all things. In a Waldorf school, the children don't usually need this explained. The image speaks for itself: the bright ribbons, the woven pattern, the laughter, the music, the smell of crushed grass and flowers — and the sense that the community itself is being woven and renewed in the dance.

The wider celebration

May Day is usually a full-school festival open to families and friends. Children wear flower crowns they have made — ivy or raffia rings woven with fresh blossoms. There may be a playful drama in which King Winter and Lady Spring meet and Winter is finally chased away. Older grades often dance more complex weaves; younger children dance simpler patterns or scatter petals. There is singing, picnicking, sometimes Morris dancing in the English tradition, May baskets of flowers left in secret on neighbours' doorsteps, and games for everyone.

How it is celebrated
  • The Maypole — a tall pole with long, coloured ribbons — set up on the school grounds
  • Children weave their way around the pole in patterned dances, braiding the ribbons
  • Flower crowns made from fresh blossoms, ivy, and raffia
  • A playful enactment of the meeting and parting of King Winter and Lady Spring (or the May Queen and May King)
  • Whole-school singing, picnics, and outdoor games
  • May baskets — small bundles of flowers left in secret on doorsteps
  • Sometimes English Morris dancing performed by an older grade

VIII.Midsummer & St John's — the festival of fullness and the bonfire

Northern hemisphere 21–24 June, at the summer solstice
Southern hemisphere 21–24 December, at the southern summer solstice

At the very crown of the year — the longest day, the shortest night, the sun at its highest — the Waldorf year closes with a final festival of light, this time the great outer light of midsummer itself.

The summer solstice marks the moment when the earth has fully breathed out — leaves wide open, flowers at their fullest, light pouring down for the longest hours. The Christian calendar places Saint John's Day here, on 24 June — the feast day of John the Baptist, whose call to "make straight the way" matches the festival's sense of clearing, renewing, and turning toward what is to come. After this day, the light begins, almost imperceptibly, to wane again.

The bonfire

Where Christmas has its candle and the lantern walk has its small flame, Midsummer has the bonfire — a great outdoor fire, often lit at dusk as the longest day finally ends. Children leap over the embers in some traditions; in others the fire simply burns while the community gathers around it, singing, eating, and watching the sparks rise into the slowly darkening sky. Many Waldorf schools hold this festival as the closing event of the school year, sending the children out into summer with the image of fire and light still warming their hearts.

The inner gesture

If Michaelmas was the kindling of inner light against gathering dark, Midsummer is the opposite gesture: a celebration of light at its fullest, freely given, joyfully shared. It is a festival of generosity — the year, the earth, and the sun pouring themselves out without holding back. The community gathers, the fire burns, the children play long into the late summer evening, and the school year ends in light.

Midsummer in the southern hemisphere For southern families, Midsummer falls at Christmas. December 21–24 is the longest day of the year. The southern Midsummer bonfire is a Christmas bonfire; the long light evenings are the very evenings of the Christmas season. This is one of the most striking gifts of the southern Waldorf year — to feel that the European Christmas tradition is actually a midwinter festival of inner light, and that your Christmas, lived in heat and abundance and the year's fullest expansion, asks for a different inner gesture: gratitude, generosity, the freely-given light of midsummer itself. Many southern Waldorf families mark both — a quiet candlelit gathering on the longest night in June, and a sunlit outdoor festival on the longest day in December.
How it is celebrated
  • A great outdoor bonfire lit at dusk on or near the summer solstice
  • Singing, dancing, and storytelling around the fire
  • Wreaths of midsummer flowers — St John's wort, daisies, wildflowers
  • Sometimes leaping over the embers (in older traditions)
  • Community feasting and picnicking outdoors
  • Often the closing festival of the school year, especially in northern-hemisphere schools
The wheel turns, and the children turn with it. Courage gathers them at the door of autumn; compassion walks them into the dark; their own small lights carry them through midwinter; joy crowns them again in spring; and the great fire of midsummer sends them back out into the world.

IX.Materials for the Festival Year

A short note on where to source the materials Waldorf families gather for the festivals — particularly the silks, candles, beeswax sheets, and ribbons that recur from one celebration to the next.

Silks — the thread that runs through the year

Of all the festival materials, silks are the most quietly important. Lanterns are sometimes covered in silk; the Advent spiral may be lined with golden silk; the May Day ribbons are silk in many schools; the seasonal table — that small altar of the year in every Waldorf home — is dressed in silks that change colour with the season (soft greens of spring, deep blues of winter, warm reds of autumn). A small basket of dyed silks is one of the simplest gifts you can give a Waldorf home; it appears in countless festival images for good reason.

Dharma Trading Company (dharmatrading.com) is genuinely the canonical source. They sell undyed habotai silks at fair prices — the 22-inch silk square is the standard playsilk size in Waldorf homes — plus the natural fabric dyes and instructions to colour them yourself. Many Waldorf families dye their own silks in small batches each season, using turmeric for yellow, madder for pink, indigo for blue, onion skins for ochre. The dyed silks then appear on the seasonal table, in the dressing-up basket, in the lantern walk, and as ribbons on the Maypole. Dharma ships internationally and is, by a wide margin, the most-recommended silk source by Waldorf parents worldwide.

If you don't wish to dye your own, Sarah's Silks (sarahssilks.com) sells ready-dyed Waldorf-quality playsilks in beautiful seasonal colours. Outside North America, Beautiful Silks (Australia) and Whaleys of Bradford (UK) are reliable alternatives.

Candles & beeswax

For the Advent spiral, Martinmas lanterns, festival tables, and birthday rituals — pure beeswax candles are the Waldorf standard. The honey-warm light and the gentle scent are part of what makes these festivals feel as they do. Recommended sources:

  • A Child's Dream (achildsdream.com) — pure beeswax candles, advent candles, taper candles, and the beeswax sheets used for rolling your own candles with children
  • Bella Luna Toys and Acorns & Twigs — similar selection
  • Myriad Natural Toys (UK) and Conscious Craft (UK) for European families
  • Local beekeepers — almost always the best option, both in quality and in supporting local apiculture

Lantern-making supplies (Martinmas)

  • Glass jars with handles — kept from kitchen use, decorated with translucent paper, tissue, or autumn leaves
  • Translucent kite paper (also called window star paper, by Stockmar) — for the most beautiful traditional lanterns; available from A Child's Dream and Mercurius
  • Wire and willow for traditional handmade lantern frames
  • Tea-light candles or small beeswax candles — battery tea lights are sometimes used for younger children's safety

Maypole & spring festival ribbons

For schools or families running their own May Day celebration, long ribbons (3–5 metres each, in soft natural colours) are wound around a central pole as the children dance. Dharma Trading sells undyed silk ribbon by the yard; many schools dye their own. Otherwise, Mercurius International sells Maypole ribbons ready-cut and finished.

Books on the festival year

  • Stephanie Cooper, Christine Fynes-Clinton & Marije Rowling — The Children's Year. Seasonal craft projects organised by month; one of the most beloved Waldorf festival books
  • Brigitte Barz — Festivals with Children. A deep, philosophical reflection on what each festival is for and how to celebrate it
  • Jennifer Aulie & Margaret Meyerkort — The Wynstones Press Festivals series. Small books for each festival with verses, songs, and stories
  • Diana Carey & Judy Large — Festivals, Family and Food. Recipes, songs, crafts, verses, gathered by season

A note: festivals can become elaborate if one wants them to be, but they need not be. A single silk on the seasonal table, a single beeswax candle in the lantern, a single sprig of fresh greens at midwinter — done with attention, year after year — is more than enough for a child to feel the shape of the year. The materials matter less than the rhythm of returning to them.
